# Where Scientists Draw the Line on AI Agents

## Summary

Based on interviews with 17 US-based scientists across the natural,
applied, and social sciences, the authors identify five dimensions of
"scientific trust" in AI research agents: validation, privacy, objectivity,
accountability, and transparency — researchers want to know *how* an answer
was reached, not just whether it looks correct. Scientists reported
verifying AI outputs against original sources, watching for subtle errors,
and worrying that retrieval tools might surface only studies fitting a
favored hypothesis. The authors argue scientists should shape agent design
and evaluation directly, proposing benchmarks that test whether agents
ignore contradictory evidence, access files outside their authorized scope,
or match established human workflows. Where researchers draw the line
varies by field and by individual: one biologist refused AI-generated
research ideas outright, while others welcomed AI help with ideation or
writing; formulaic sections like methods descriptions were seen as more
suitable for automation than substantive content, and using AI to critique
one's own manuscript was viewed more favorably than using it on a
colleague's unpublished work. The authors note safeguards cut both ways:
content restrictions can block legitimate research on sensitive topics
(e.g., political violence), while sensitive human-subject data calls for
stronger privacy and access controls. The study is explicitly exploratory
rather than a representative survey or an evaluation of specific agents.

## Why it matters

Names a usable five-dimension framework (validation, privacy, objectivity,
accountability, transparency) for anyone designing or evaluating AI agents
intended for research use, plus concrete, field-sensitive examples of where
researchers currently draw automation lines — relevant both to building
more trustworthy research-agent products (ai_engineering lens 8) and to the
underlying question of what makes AI-mediated research outputs trustworthy
or knowable (social_science lens 8). The findings are qualitative and from a
small, exploratory sample, so they inform rather than settle design
questions.
