# Data-Center Growth Impacts Household Electricity Costs More Through Wholesale Prices Than Through Grid Reinforcement

## Summary
Models 371 announced Texas data-center projects, totaling 38.9 GW of demand
by 2030, in an integrated framework linking power-system expansion,
wholesale electricity markets, transmission reinforcement, and retail cost
allocation. The central finding: the residential cost burden from
data-center growth is driven mainly by higher wholesale electricity prices,
not by direct transmission (grid-reinforcement) investment — about 68% of
the cost increase comes from wholesale prices versus about 32% from
regulated transmission charges in the baseline scenario. Quantified
scenarios: a baseline case adds 1.64 cents/kWh to residential rates;
allowing new gas generation to site near data centers lowers this to 0.92
cents/kWh; excluding data centers from the transmission cost-sharing base
raises it to 1.87 cents/kWh. The outcome depends on where new generation is
sited, how infrastructure is planned, and how costs are allocated between
data centers and residential ratepayers (model's internal validation and
full methodology not read at capture — unverified beyond the abstract).

## Why it matters
Gives a concrete, scenario-quantified answer to a policy question regulators
are actively litigating — who pays for the electricity data centers draw —
and names the specific levers (generation siting, cost-sharing-base
exclusion) that shift the residential cost impact by roughly a factor of
two, directly usable by anyone evaluating AI-datacenter electricity-rate
policy or cost-allocation proposals.
