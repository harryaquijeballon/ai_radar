# A Task-Based Approach to Generative AI: Evidence from a Field Experiment in Central Banking

## Summary

A field experiment at the National Bank of Slovakia randomly assigned generative AI access to central bank employees completing workplace tasks designed to mirror the theoretical task-based framework. The authors report that generative AI access "leads to large improvements in both quality and efficiency for the majority of participants," and find "a strong complementarity between generative AI and non-routine work, both on average and for most participants."

The paper's distinguishing result is a mismatch between task-level and worker-level effects: workers in routine jobs experience larger individual performance gains, yet generative AI is *less* effective for the routine task content of their work. The authors describe this task-versus-worker mismatch as "economically large," and report that a simulation exercise implies "the organization can increase output by 7.3% by changing how workers are assigned to tasks in the presence of generative AI" — that is, from reallocation alone, without further technology adoption.

The paper also reports a skill asymmetry in how benefits arrive: low-skill workers benefit most in terms of quality, while high-skill workers benefit in terms of efficiency. Support is found for generative AI being cognitive-biased and specialist-biased, though the authors state these effects are smaller in magnitude than their tests of routine-bias.

## Why it matters

Most organisational evidence on generative AI measures whether individual workers get faster or better. This measures something more useful for anyone advising an organisation on deployment: that the gains depend on *which tasks sit with which workers*, and that a substantial share of the available benefit — 7.3% of output in this setting — is unlocked by reassignment rather than by adoption. For a research organisation weighing how to deploy AI across its own analysts, that reframes the question from "who gets access" to "who should be doing what once access exists."

The routine-worker paradox is the specific finding to carry forward: the workers who gain most individually are not the workers whose *task content* the technology serves best. Any productivity projection built on worker-level gains alone will therefore misattribute where the value comes from. The low-skill-quality versus high-skill-efficiency split is a further concrete, testable claim for distributional work on AI and labour.
