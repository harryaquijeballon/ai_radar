# Prizes, Shared Hazard, and the Speed of Frontier AI

## Summary

Models frontier AI labs as racing for commercial leadership while each
bearing only part of the catastrophe risk ("hazard externality") that its own
speed creates. Using an illustrative, calibrated three-lab takeoff model, the
paper compares racing against a common research-share rule, decomposing the
contribution of two forces — the contest for leadership and the shared
hazard — by removing each in turn. It also examines more-lab scenarios, an
idealized fully-open-source regime, and the real September 2026 White House
Accord on Super Intelligence. In the model: racing reaches takeoff in 18
months versus 69 months under a common-share rule; removing the contest
closes most of the catastrophe-probability gap (over 90% when full-speed
catastrophe probability is 75%, 69% at 25%; averaged over removal order, the
contest explains 65% and the shared hazard 44% of the two cases
respectively); adding more labs makes racing worse; under ideal open source,
labs voluntarily slow to the coordinated pace; one lab's unilateral restraint
achieves far less than a common rule; and the Accord, which audits safety but
sets no speed limit, does not slow the race in the model.

## Why it matters

Gives AI-governance policy debate a quantified answer to "how much of AI
racing is really about catastrophic risk-sharing versus the commercial
contest itself?" — directly actionable for anyone arguing for or evaluating
research-sharing rules, speed limits, or audit-only accords like the actual
September 2026 White House Accord on Super Intelligence, which the paper
argues (within its model) does not change racing incentives because it
imposes no speed constraint.
