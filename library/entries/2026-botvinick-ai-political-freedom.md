# What Will You Build For: Matthew Botvinick

## Summary

An interview/essay for the Cosmos Institute's "What Will You Build For" series, by Matthew Botvinick — a senior fellow at Yale Law School and Cosmos Institute, author of *AI and Political Freedom*, writing in a personal capacity. Published 6 November 2026.

Main thesis: AI does not change democracy in kind, it amplifies existing opportunities and vulnerabilities, and its chief danger is making democratic backsliding more likely and harder to reverse. The author argues society needs to reorganize political life around AI rather than rely on alignment and regulation alone.

Key claims: AI pressures the authoritarian playbook through labour-driven economic insecurity that can fuel populism, and through AI-enabled surveillance justified by security crises. A long-run concern is a "political resource curse": if AI ends most work, governments may lose the accountability that comes from depending on broad labour taxation. Conversely, AI-scaled citizen assemblies and deliberative polling ("Athens at scale") could enable more direct, participatory governance. On autonomy and epistemics, the piece questions whether AI that writes political content or supplies information represents users faithfully, and whether it can stay neutral the way a newspaper is supposed to. The author criticizes the AI community for over-relying on alignment and regulation and under-reading the history of technology, corporate power, and politics.

Practical proposals offered: improve everyday government services to build public confidence; pass surveillance reforms; limit executive emergency powers; treat AI prompts and outputs as federal records; regulate AI procurement; strengthen congressional and judicial oversight. The essay draws a historical analogy between current populist anger at data centers and Progressive-era anger at railroads, arguing the outcome (reform vs. authoritarian drift) depends on public trust in democratic institutions, which the author describes as very low.

## Why it matters

Gives the social-science audience a concrete, named framework (the "political resource curse," "Athens at scale") and a specific policy menu (federal-records treatment of AI prompts, AI procurement regulation) for the AI-and-democratic-institutions debate, rather than generic alarm or dismissal — directly counterbalancing the economics-heavy lenses of this profile with political-theory grounding (lens 7) and an explicit treatment of AI's effect on political autonomy and epistemic trust (lens 8).
