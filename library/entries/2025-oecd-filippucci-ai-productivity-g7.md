# Macroeconomic productivity gains from Artificial Intelligence in G7 economies

## Summary

OECD working paper (AI Papers No. 41, June 2025) projecting AI's contribution to annual labour productivity growth in G7 economies over a 10-year horizon. These are scenario-dependent projections from a calibrated framework — not observed causal effects.

**Method (the paper's own):** a micro-to-macro framework adapting Acemoglu (2024) to sectors. Sector productivity growth = micro-level task gains × sectoral AI exposure × expected 10-year adoption increase (equation 1, §2, p. 9); sector gains aggregate to macro via value-added shares (Hulten approximation), converted to labour productivity with a 1.5 capital-deepening multiplier (§3, p. 21, fns 24–25).

**Imported assumptions:** average micro-level gain of 30% TFP at task level, a conservative average of the three most precise experimental estimates from the literature (range across studies 14–56%; §2.1, pp. 9–10); task exposure from Eloundou et al. (2024), in baseline and "expanded capabilities" variants (§2.2, pp. 10–11).

**The paper's own empirical contribution:** harmonised estimates of current high-intensity AI adoption in core business functions — much lower than headline "any use" figures (2024: EU ~3% vs 14%; US 4% vs 9%; §2.3, pp. 13–15) — yielding country rates from 1.9% (Japan) to 6.1% (US) (fn 19, p. 16).

**Scenario assumptions:** three S-curve adoption paths benchmarked to past general-purpose technologies — slow (electricity), medium (computers/internet), rapid (mobile phones) — fitted as logistic curves with an 80% long-run ceiling (§2.3.2, pp. 16–18, fn 23), crossed with baseline vs expanded AI capabilities.

**Projected ranges (Table 2, p. 24):** annual labour-productivity contribution over the next decade of 0.4–1.3 pp in high-exposure, high-adoption economies (US 0.41–1.28; UK 0.39–1.27) and 0.2–0.8 pp where determinants are less favourable (Italy 0.19–0.89; Japan 0.16–0.82). Cross-country differences are driven mainly by sectoral composition — the five most AI-exposed knowledge-intensive service sectors are 15–25% of value added across the G7 (Figure 5, p. 13) — and by adoption levels.

**Authors' interpretation and limitations:** short-run measured productivity may lag or dip (J-curve; §3.2, p. 25); Baumol-type reallocation is unlikely to cut aggregate gains by more than ~one-sixth in most scenarios, per the predecessor paper's GE model (Box 1, pp. 22–23); UK and Japan estimates carry extra uncertainty because adoption there is regression-predicted, not surveyed (fn 5, p. 7); upside (organisational innovation) and downside ("bad tasks", AI misuse) risks are acknowledged (§2.4, p. 20).

**Policy implications (authors'):** digital infrastructure, STEM/AI skills, competition, easing labour and capital reallocation across sectors, and — for measurement — harmonised AI-adoption surveys (§4, p. 26).

## Why it matters

*(Radar's assessment, not the paper's.)* This is the reference benchmark for "how much productivity can AI plausibly add" in advanced economies — the number set most likely to be quoted in policy debates a telecom/digital economist participates in. Two immediately practical uses: the harmonised high-intensity adoption rates (a corrective to headline adoption figures, directly relevant when assessing AI-diffusion claims in the digital economy) and the transparent exposure × adoption × micro-gain method, which is reusable as a template for sector-level analysis in other industries or country groups.
