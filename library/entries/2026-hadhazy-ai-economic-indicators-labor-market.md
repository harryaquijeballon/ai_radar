# Tracking AI's Real-Time Footprint On The Labor Market

## Summary
Stanford HAI's Digital Economy Lab (DEL) runs the "AI Economic Indicators"
project, a set of monthly dashboards launched June 2026 meant to track AI's
economic effects faster than official statistics. The article describes
three dashboards: the **Transformation Tracker** (12 macro indicators,
currently showing no sign of explosive AI-driven growth; DEL lead Connacher
Murphy flags elevated 10-year interest rates as the most responsive, if only
mildly suggestive, signal), the **Canaries Dashboard** (built with ADP
Research on anonymized payroll data, grouping employment by occupational AI
exposure, based on the "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" study by Bharat Chandar
and colleagues), and the **Adoption Monitor** (survey-based tracking of
generative-AI adoption by individuals and firms). A newer dashboard, **GDP-B:
Surplus Observer** (led by David Nguyen), estimates consumer surplus from AI
tools via online surveys.

Headline claims from the Canaries Dashboard: employment in AI-exposed
occupations (software development, customer service) is down more than 4%
this year; among 22-25-year-olds in those occupations, employment has fallen
over 3% annually since November 2022, with the decline sharpening to 4.4%
recently. Occupations skewed toward automation (vs. augmentation) show
slower or shrinking employment growth, especially for early-career workers.
The dashboard also shows a hint of slower job growth for young women than
young men, which Chandar cautions may reflect ADP's non-representative
sample rather than a real gap, since longer-run government statistics show
none; DEL plans to keep watching it (unverified pending further data).
Automation-vs-augmentation classification of AI usage draws on the Anthropic
Economic Index's occupation-tagged analysis of roughly a million anonymized
Claude conversations.

## Why it matters
This is a citable, updating measurement source for AI's labor-market effects
distinct from one-off papers or vendor usage data: a researcher working on
AI-and-employment questions can cite the dashboards directly, track them
monthly, and use the automation/augmentation occupational split as a
framework. The caveats the DEL team themselves raise (ADP sample
representativeness, no causal identification yet, "no explosive growth" in
the macro aggregates despite labor-market movement) are also directly usable
for productivity-measurement-debate framing (lens 1's J-curve/micro-macro gap
theme).
