# The Nvidia-Sized Hole in US GDP Statistics

## Summary

Argues that US GDP statistics undercount the value created by Nvidia and
other "fabless" chipmakers, because national accounts have no category that
captures it: Nvidia designs chips in the US, has them manufactured abroad
(mainly by TSMC in Taiwan), and sells the finished chips to foreign server
makers — so no goods or IP formally cross the US border, and Nvidia's
markup is invisible on the export side even though the matching import
(finished servers bought by US data centers) is fully subtracted from GDP.
The authors systematically rule out every plausible national-accounts
category (goods exports, IP exports, service exports, merchanting, other
services) as a home for this value, and got the Bureau of Economic Analysis
to confirm in writing that the goods "do not cross the US border" and so
are undocumented in customs records. Using Nvidia's US-booked operating
income (~$120bn pre-tax, Jan 2025–Jan 2026; ~$30bn/quarter) as a proxy for
the missing value-add, they estimate recent US GDP growth has been
understated by about 0.3 percentage points — "today's largest measurement
issue attributable to a single firm" — a gap that could widen to almost 2
percentage points by 2028 if Nvidia's income keeps growing exponentially.
They note a related oddity: US investment in computing equipment (~$400bn/
year, nearly triple 2023 levels) is now almost exactly offset by imports of
the same goods, making the computing boom register as contributing "roughly
zero" to measured GDP. IMF guidance (BPM-7, 2025) already recommends
treating factoryless manufacturers' overseas sales as exports, but the BEA
has "no plans" to adopt this soon; prior comparable guideline updates took
about five years to implement.

## Why it matters

A specific, quantified reason to distrust headline US GDP-growth figures as
a gauge of AI's macroeconomic contribution right now, with a stated and
growing magnitude (0.3pp today, ~2pp by 2028) rather than a vague caveat —
directly usable by anyone modeling or citing AI's effect on measured
growth (lens 1), and a concrete illustration of a real productivity/
measurement gap (as opposed to the more familiar "AI productivity
J-curve" mismeasurement story) that a researcher could incorporate or cite
as a correction term.
