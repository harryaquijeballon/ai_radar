# AI and the Two Margins of Work: Why Job Losses Show Up Before Job Creation

## Summary

Argues that technological change affects labour demand through two
margins: it changes what existing establishments do, and it creates new
activity, often through new firms. Payroll statistics capture the first
margin quickly but the second with a long lag, because new work shows up
in the data only once new establishments are large enough to register.
Using data from the computing era, the authors find that firms founded in
1978 or later held 42% of payroll by 1998, and that in the industries most
exposed to automation, incumbent firms shrank before new entrants grew to
replace the lost activity. They build a model in which AI lowers the cost
of acceptable output in existing production and makes previously
unprofitable new projects worth attempting; depending on how quickly
operations rescale, hours may fall first and recover only later, with the
long-run effect depending on income effects. Using establishment job-flow
data through Q1 2026, they report no relationship between language-model
exposure and job flows on either margin over that period. They propose a
"net reinstatement ratio" and argue that because statistics record new
work late, displacement is mechanically overstated for years after a
technology's introduction. (unverified: the Q1 2026 null-result finding and
the "net reinstatement ratio" construction are as stated in the abstract;
full paper not read at capture.)

## Why it matters

For economists tracking AI-and-labour debates: a concrete methodological
warning that current payroll/establishment statistics will systematically
overstate AI-driven displacement for years, simply because job creation on
the new-activity margin is recorded later than job loss on the
existing-activity margin — relevant to interpreting any near-term
"AI killed jobs" headline number. The proposed "net reinstatement ratio" is
a usable construct for researchers wanting to correct for this
measurement lag rather than take raw job-flow numbers at face value.
