# So You Think the World Is About to Change?

## Summary

The authors argue that early AI-safety thinkers and researchers, having
succeeded in raising existential-risk concerns (crediting the effective
altruist movement's influence from around 2014), must now shift from
theoretical diagnosis to institution-building for an AI-transformed world.
They draw an extended historical analogy to the Saint-Simonians of 1830s
Paris, whose utopian religious movement collapsed but whose members went on
to build lasting railways and financial institutions — arguing "the
experience accumulated along the way is itself valuable." They contend
narrow AI-safety institutions (citing DeepMind and Anthropic) are
"necessary but not sufficient," and that new institutions must (1) address
emerging problems such as "machines can generate papers faster than people
can referee them" (citing NeurIPS's own management of AI-generated
submissions), (2) be staffed with genuine domain expertise rather than
generalist AI-safety credentials, (3) work within near-term technological
constraints instead of an end-state vision, and (4) avoid uncompromising
theoretical purity — illustrated via Thomas Paine's marginalization after
attacking imperfect allies, and via Chesterton's Fence. Meta's Muse (a
consumer agentic AI product that filters information per user
specification) is cited as a live example of the kind of "attention
guardian" institution the authors argue is needed.

## Why it matters

A normative argument rather than an empirical result: it offers researchers
and policy readers a framework — institution-building over theoretical
purity, with the Saint-Simonians and NeurIPS's own submission-management
response as worked examples — for thinking about what comes after
diagnosing AI risk. This is exactly the vocabulary lens 8 exists to track:
sharpening how trust, autonomy, and institutional design in an AI-mediated
world get reasoned about, even though the piece argues by analogy rather
than evidence and offers no testable claim of its own.
