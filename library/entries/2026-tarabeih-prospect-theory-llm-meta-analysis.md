# Prospect Theory in Large Language Models: A Meta-Analysis of Loss Aversion, Diminishing Sensitivity, and Probability Weighting

## Summary

A meta-analysis pooling 102 fitted estimates from 11 studies covering 32
language models, testing whether LLMs reproduce the three components of
prospect theory found in human decision-making: loss aversion, diminishing
sensitivity, and probability weighting. Reported findings: loss aversion is
modest — pooled λ = 1.26 (95% CI 1.06–1.46), well below the strongest
human estimate of 1.96; under symmetric gain/loss conditions LLMs (λ =
1.15) are close to humans (1.07), but when losses are smaller than gains,
LLMs (1.46) fall well short of humans (2.3). Diminishing sensitivity
depends heavily on elicitation method: under the Tversky–Kahneman method
the value function is "nearly linear" (1.01 vs. 0.67 for humans), while
under the Tanaka method it is more concave than in humans (0.52).
Probability weighting is reported to resemble humans, with an inverse
S-shaped distortion (γ = 0.81 under Tversky–Kahneman, 0.84 under Tanaka),
though the author notes this rests on few studies. Overall: LLMs show
little loss aversion and little diminishing sensitivity but retain
human-like probability distortion, with patterns varying across model
families and fitting methods (all figures as stated in the fetched
abstract; underlying per-study data not independently reviewed).

## Why it matters

A specific, quantified map of where LLM "economic behaviour" diverges from
human behaviour and where it doesn't — directly relevant to anyone using
LLMs as simulated subjects in behavioural or experimental economics
("homo silicus" work). The method-dependence finding (Tversky-Kahneman vs.
Tanaka elicitation giving opposite conclusions about diminishing
sensitivity) is a concrete, actionable warning: a validity problem tied to
elicitation method choice, not just to the model.
