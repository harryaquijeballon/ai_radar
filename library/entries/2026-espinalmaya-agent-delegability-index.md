# The Delegation Divide: An Open Index of AI-Agent Delegability across Occupations and Countries

## Summary
The paper builds the Agent Delegability Index (ADI), a measure of how much
work can be handed to AI agents, by occupation and by country — extending
earlier generative-AI exposure measures (mostly US-only) to a global scale.
Two language models rated all 18,796 task statements in O*NET 30.2 against a
frozen, preregistered rubric of delegation levels and barriers; tasks were
mapped to 433 ISCO-08 unit groups and weighted by ILOSTAT employment data for
146 countries. Reliability checks: inter-model agreement κ = 0.77, a blind
human rating of 300 tasks at κ = 0.85, and an agentic panel with web search
at κ = 0.87.

Headline finding: 35.2% of task time in world employment is at least partly
delegable in 2026, but only 2.5% can be fully delegated. Delegable
employment rises with income — from 22.5% in low-income countries to 48.7%
in high-income countries, a slope of 7.23 percentage points per log point of
GDP per capita — which the authors attribute to the job mix poorer countries
employ most (dominated by physical-task barriers), not to differential AI
capability. At the occupation level, ADI closely tracks existing exposure
measures (r = 0.93 with Eloundou et al., r = 0.89 with Felten et al.) while
adding delegation-level and barrier detail. Full delegation is rare and
peaks for tasks taking a human about 11.8 minutes. A preregistered hypothesis
that delegability and observed AI usage would diverge more in middle-income
countries was *not* supported: across 101 countries the two correlate at
r = 0.86 with no significant gap by income group (p = 0.27).

## Why it matters
This gives social-science and policy audiences a citable, methodologically
transparent delegability measure that goes beyond the US-only exposure
literature this profile already tracks (Eloundou, Felten) — directly usable
for cross-country development and labour-market analysis (lens 2), and for
framing growth/productivity debates about how much of measured "exposure"
could actually be acted on today versus later (lens 1). The negative result
on the adoption-gap hypothesis is itself a usable finding: it cautions
against assuming AI diffusion will be systematically slower, relative to
technical delegability, in middle-income economies.
