# Structural economic change under the digital transformation

## Summary

Uses a calibrated agent-based model of the global economy to compare two
scenarios of digital-input-driven productivity growth with the same
cumulative productivity increase: one where digital inputs function as
ordinary intermediate goods ("low embeddedness"), and one where they are
combined directly with primary factors of production ("high
embeddedness"). The paper's opening claim, quoted directly: "Productivity
improvements in digital industries could massively reshape the structure
of production networks." Both scenarios raise gross output, household
utility, and GDP across economies, but value-added gains are smaller and
unevenly spread across sectors than the output gains suggest. High
embeddedness strengthens production responses, especially in industries
most exposed to digital inputs, and as digital industries become more
central they draw labour from other sectors, pushing those sectors further
upstream or downstream in the production network. The authors warn that
persistent labour reallocation of this kind could leave workers unemployed
if they cannot move into the expanding industries. (unverified: the exact
magnitudes of the output/value-added gap and the sectoral reallocation
patterns are as summarized from the abstract; full model and calibration
not read at capture.)

## Why it matters

For economists tracking AI/digital effects on growth: a reusable modelling
distinction — whether digital inputs are "ordinary intermediate goods" or
"combined directly with primary factors" — that changes the predicted
distribution of gains (output vs. value-added) and labour-reallocation risk
across sectors. That is a concrete choice researchers building their own
growth/production-network models of AI or digital diffusion could adopt or
test against, and a mechanism for why headline GDP/output gains from
AI-driven productivity growth may overstate value-added gains for workers.
