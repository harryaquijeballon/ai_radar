# Accuracy Is Not Welfare: Evaluating AI Buyers under Strategic Price Complexity in a Synthetic Duopoly

## Summary
Studies how AI buying agents change sellers' pricing incentives and
consumers' outcomes in a synthetic duopoly, testing two price-comparison
pipelines — calculator-assisted comparison and verified-total disclosure
(where sellers must reveal verified total prices) — on a fixed menu of
offers and in empirically grounded correlated-equilibrium games. Calculator
assistance picked the objectively optimal offer 84.00% of the time versus
68.57% for verified-total disclosure, making it the more accurate pipeline.
Despite being less accurate, disclosure produced a 20.14-unit higher
consumer-worst-case equilibrium surplus — the better welfare outcome — and a
frozen-strategy holdout comparison also favored disclosure. However, when
equilibria were re-solved under scenario resampling, the resulting range for
disclosure's welfare advantage spanned zero, meaning that advantage is not
robust under that test (full methodology and resampling procedure not read
at capture — unverified beyond the abstract).

## Why it matters
A concrete demonstration that a more "accurate" AI agent (by a standard
decision-accuracy metric) can still leave the people it acts for worse off,
and that the opposite — the welfare-superior design — only surfaces when
accuracy, loss severity, seller incentives, and welfare are measured
separately. Directly usable by anyone evaluating AI shopping/procurement
agents (regulatory angle) or designing agent evaluation metrics more
broadly (engineering angle): accuracy-only evaluation can silently mask a
worse real-world outcome.
