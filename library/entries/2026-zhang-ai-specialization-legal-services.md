# AI, Specialization, and the Organization of Knowledge Work: Evidence from Legal Services

## Summary

Argues that cheaper AI-assisted expertise does not automatically move
professional-service work in-house or lower premium prices; it can instead
reallocate work among outside providers and self-service. Using a
difference-in-differences design across 89 U.S. federal districts
(non-prisoner civil cases vs. prisoner petitions, AO Table C-13 panel data,
FY2010–2025), the authors find self-representation rose 6.6 percentage
points (SE 0.9) relative to the comparison group by FY2025 — 5.7 points
after adjusting for a modest pre-existing trend — with the non-prisoner
self-represented share reaching 16.8% in FY2025 after sitting between 9.5%
and 12.1% for nearly two decades. Increases were largest in case types
requiring drafting a complaint (civil rights cases rose from 26.1% to
38.5%). Real household spending on legal services (BEA) stayed 12.1% above
its 2010–2019 trend in 2025, so self-representation did not coincide with
falling paid legal demand. Using ACS microdata (42-state panel), the
in-house share of private-sector lawyers moved only from 28.7% (2019) to
29.0% (2024), with no statistically clear post-2022 acceleration and no
significant association between industry-level AI adoption and in-house
lawyer employment (β = −0.004 per percentage point, p = 0.79). The Am Law
100 reported 13.0% gross revenue growth in 2025 against only 0.1% growth in
billable hours at 50 of those firms, with worked rates up about 9%; lower-
priced midsize firms showed stronger hours growth. Across 41 firms with
matched survey data, lower revenue per lawyer correlated with stronger
self-reported AI integration (Spearman 0.32, p = 0.04). The authors note
legal-AI capability on basic reasoning has risen sharply (LegalBench best
scores ~64% in early 2023 to ~89% in October 2026) while harder multi-step
tasks remain difficult (Harvey Legal Agent Benchmark all-pass rate ~7% in
May to ~25% in October 2026). The authors explicitly caution the analysis
does not isolate AI's causal contribution, and disclose employment by
micro1, which produces three of the cited legal-AI benchmarks — a
disclosure that does not extend to the paper's independent court,
employment, spending, and revenue data.

## Why it matters

For economists tracking AI-and-labour-market debates: concrete,
multi-source evidence that a "cheaper expertise" shock reallocates
professional-service work along several margins at once (client self-help,
supplier competition, pricing, hours) rather than producing the simple
in-house-shift or price-collapse predictions common in AI-and-automation
commentary. The paper's recommendation — track work composition and
customer selection across suppliers and within-service prices, rather than
relying on specialist headcount or average billing rates alone — is a
directly applicable methodological point for anyone measuring AI's labour
market effects in other professional-service sectors.
