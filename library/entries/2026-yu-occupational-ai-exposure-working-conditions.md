# Dull, Dirty, Dangerous? Working Conditions and the Limits of Occupational AI Exposure

## Summary

Tests whether occupations with worse working conditions (dull, dirty, or
dangerous — the conditions often cited to justify automation) have higher
occupational AI exposure. Using 21 O*NET items across 754 US occupations, the
author finds that, unadjusted, higher AI exposure is associated with lower
working-condition scores under both an LLM-based exposure measure and the
established AI Occupational Exposure (AIOE) measure, whether occupations are
weighted equally or by employment. Once the model adjusts for physical
demands, required education, training and experience, and wages, the
composite association weakens: the adjusted confidence interval for the
LLM-based exposure measure includes zero, while the AIOE-based interval stays
below zero. Separating "repetition" from "restricted discretion" within the
composite "Dull" score changes what that combined score is capturing. The
paper concludes that occupational exposure profiles are useful for selecting
research settings, but that establishing whether AI delivers desired
automation or realized relief from bad conditions requires direct evidence on
worker preferences and specific interventions, not exposure scores alone.

## Why it matters

A direct, quantified check on a claim economists and commentators make
routinely — that AI will relieve workers of dull, dirty, or dangerous work —
using two independently constructed exposure measures side by side. The
finding that the association survives for one measure (AIOE) but not the
other (LLM-based) once confounds are controlled is itself a usable caution
for anyone citing occupational-AI-exposure scores as evidence of welfare
effects: the scores are useful for sampling occupations, not for inferring
whether AI actually improves job quality (lens 1).
