# The Adoption of Artificial Intelligence in Firms: New Evidence for Policymaking

## Summary

A book-length OECD/BCG/INSEAD study (196 pages) of how firms adopt AI and how governments can support adoption. The core is "a policy-oriented survey of 840 enterprises implemented in the Group of Seven (G7) countries – the 2022-23 OECD/BCG/INSEAD Survey of AI-Adopting Enterprises - plus 167 enterprises in Brazil" (São Paulo state, run with the Brazilian Network Information Centre). The G7 survey ran November 2022 – January 2023; the Brazil survey February – July 2023. Complements: a literature review of prior adoption surveys (Chapter 2, drafted by ITIF), structured interviews with 19 technology-diffusion institutions in G7 countries and Singapore (e.g. Germany's Fraunhofer IAO/IPA, the UK's Digital Catapult, the US Manufacturing Extension Partnership), and interviews with C-suite and technical leaders in adopting firms.

Principal findings as stated in the executive summary:

- **Skills policies are the most valued.** Skill scarcity hinders uptake "even in many large firms"; "policies and programmes to develop human capital are among the most valued and used by businesses." Academic certifications "may not provide all the information employers seek"; updated qualification frameworks and industry-tailored, real-world training are recommended.
- **Firms want better public data and simpler access.** Policymakers should prioritise data quality in public repositories, simplify acquisition procedures, document datasets, centralise access platforms, and "enhance the compatibility of legal frameworks governing cross-border data flows."
- **University collaboration is widespread and valued but could be enhanced** — via first-collaboration financial support, R&D tax incentives designed for industry-university work, and simpler application processes for public funds.
- **Dedicated diffusion institutions help.** These institutions consistently identify uncertainty over return on investment and lack of data maturity as fundamental barriers, and report that managers "often struggle to understand how AI can address real problems in the workplace and simultaneously underestimate the enterprise-wide implications and changes in business culture that AI may entail."
- **Information and advice are an underrated policy lever.** Even advanced adopters seek signposted, current, specific guidance — regulatory updates, compliance guidance, use cases, and vendor-selection frameworks for SMEs. "Enterprises also wish to see clearly stated accountability frameworks for the safe use of AI."
- **The evidence base itself needs work.** The study calls for international comparability across national AI surveys (several NSOs helped shape the questionnaire, given in Annex C) and for evaluation of diffusion institutions' cost, scale and demonstration effects.

A material caveat stated by the study itself: "the survey was developed and administered before the November 2022 release of ChatGPT," so it captures the state of pre-generative-AI adoption as of early-to-mid 2023 and positions itself as "a strong starting point for researchers trying to ascertain the adoption trajectory of the latest family of AI technologies."

## Why it matters

This is the reference cross-country, firm-level evidence base on AI adoption for policy analysis: standardised data beyond the EU-only supranational surveys, novel policy-preference questions asked directly of enterprises, and a cognitively-tested questionnaire (Annex C) explicitly offered for reuse by national statistical offices — directly usable in connectivity/digital-adoption research design. The barriers hierarchy (ROI uncertainty and data maturity ahead of raw technology access) and the finding that generic skills programmes are valued but poorly matched to firm-specific needs are both citable framings for adoption-policy arguments in developed and developing-country contexts alike (the São Paulo replication extends the instrument beyond the G7).

Its pre-ChatGPT timing is a feature for one use and a limitation for another: it is a clean baseline of "classic" AI adoption against which generative-AI-era adoption claims can be compared, but its findings should not be quoted as evidence about generative AI adoption itself.
