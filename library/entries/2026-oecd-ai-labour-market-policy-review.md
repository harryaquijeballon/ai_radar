# Recent policy developments on AI in the labour market

## Summary

A comparative review of AI-in-the-workplace policy measures recently adopted in G7 countries and at EU level, plus four Latin American countries (Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico). The stated method combines "desk research, information collected through a dedicated questionnaire to G7 countries and the EU, and exploratory work using AI prompt engineering."

Coverage is organised into six policy areas, aligned to the OECD AI Principles and the G7 Action plan on AI in the World of Work: (1) automation, productivity, skills and opportunities; (2) privacy and data protection; (3) non-discrimination; (4) occupational safety and health; (5) transparency, explainability and accountability; and (6) social dialogue.

Principal findings as stated:

- **Adoption promotion is universal.** "All surveyed countries promote AI adoption through broad national strategies, often complemented by research and experimental initiatives, including regulatory sandboxes." Nearly all provide targeted SME support through financial incentives and training. Several have established dedicated observatories on AI in the workplace.
- **Displacement support is generic, not AI-specific.** "To assist workers displaced by AI, countries tend to rely on standard employment support policies and social protection not targeted on AI." The report identifies a specific gap: "Relatively few measures are directed at workers who remain in employment but are at high risk of AI-related disruption."
- **Reskilling skews technical.** All surveyed countries run reskilling and upskilling programmes, but "most programmes focus on technical skills, not on general AI literacy."
- **Existing frameworks are being reused rather than replaced.** In privacy, data protection, non-discrimination and OSH, countries are "first of all, making use of existing frameworks," supplemented with guidance, workshops and self-check tools; some are additionally introducing new law. "Across these areas, risk assessments are recommended or required by all countries surveyed, while certifications, audits and security measures are also widely recommended or required."
- **Transparency obligations exist but stay abstract.** Employers are generally required or encouraged to disclose AI use to workers, in some countries to job applicants, and "in a few cases, there are explicit rules for platform workers." However, transparency, explainability and accountability "often remain at the level of broad principles in AI strategies."
- **Social dialogue is promoted but under-resourced.** Engagement runs through consultation mechanisms and collective bargaining, yet "relatively few countries are investing in building AI expertise among social partners."

The report's forward look is candid about what has not yet happened: "With time, countries will also benefit from evaluating existing policy measures and assessing whether existing government guidance is used effectively and succeeds in improving workplace outcomes."

## Why it matters

This is a reference artifact rather than a result. Its value is that it answers, from an authoritative primary source with a consistent comparative structure, the question "what are governments actually doing about AI and work?" across a defined set of jurisdictions — useful when a policy claim needs grounding in what exists rather than what is proposed.

Two specific observations are more than inventory and are the parts worth carrying forward. First, the gap for workers who are *not* displaced but are at high risk of disruption: displacement policy is being handled with pre-existing generic instruments, and almost nothing is aimed at the larger population of workers whose jobs change without ending. Second, the report's own admission that policy effect has not been evaluated anywhere — meaning any claim that AI labour-market policy is "working" currently has no evaluative evidence base behind it. Both are usable framings when assessing AI-and-work policy arguments.

A methodological detail is notable given this library's own subject matter: an OECD comparative policy review now lists "exploratory work using AI prompt engineering" among its evidence-gathering methods. The report does not elaborate on how it was used or how outputs were validated, so this is recorded as an observation, not as a documented method.
