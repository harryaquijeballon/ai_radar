# AI Capability Races Ahead of the Business Payoff

## Summary

Commentary arguing that a substantial gap persists between demonstrated AI capability and measurable business productivity: despite heavy infrastructure investment and rapid capability gains, economy-wide productivity improvements have not materialised.

The load-bearing evidence is measurement of total factor productivity. Per the piece, San Francisco Fed estimates show TFP growth near zero over the past year, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics put 2025 TFP growth at 0.8 percent — matching its own ten-year average, and therefore indicating nothing extraordinary. The argument is framed by a quotation from Ernie Tedeschi (Stripe): "If AI was making workers more productive at the frontier, TFP estimates should be accelerating just like overall labor productivity. But they aren't."

The explanation offered is that adoption remains "too thin," and that firms are largely maximising existing infrastructure rather than achieving the transformative efficiency gains that would signal genuine economic integration of the technology.

## Why it matters

This is the aggregate-data counterweight to the growing body of firm- and task-level experimental evidence showing real AI productivity gains. Anyone citing micro results to project macro effects needs a defensible answer to the fact that TFP is, on these measures, flat — and this piece supplies the specific figures and the dated attribution to make that argument precisely rather than rhetorically.

The two numbers to carry forward are the near-zero SF Fed TFP growth over the past year and the BLS 2025 figure of 0.8 percent sitting exactly on its ten-year average. Together they form a mid-2026 baseline: whatever AI has done at the level of individual workers and tasks, it had not yet shown up in aggregate productivity statistics at that date. That is the empirical shape of the micro-macro gap (lens 1's J-curve debate), stated with sources.
