# Who Keeps the Gains from Personal AI Assistants? Seller Adaptation and the Unassisted in a Language-Model Market Simulation

## Summary

The authors build an agent-based rental-market simulation in which language models play consumers, assistants and adaptive sellers using algorithmic pricing. Half the population receives AI assistants operating under either an advisory or an executing mandate. Reported findings (per the arXiv abstract page): executing assistants cut adopters' spending by $13.7 per renter-day when sellers are frozen; seller adaptation recovers about one-third of that, leaving $8.7 of consumer benefit; savings appear as lower bills and as rentals completed at all; sellers raise headline rates while cutting fees; unassisted consumers see a mean spending change of +0.4 (CI -0.6 to +1.3), against a predicted +3.6 burden. The authors conclude that fee conduct, and with it the division of gains, is decided on the seller side, and that assistants should be evaluated at market level — completion rates, total spending and non-user outcomes — rather than on user outcomes alone.

## Why it matters

For researchers using LLM agents as simulated economic actors, the paper is a concrete design that tests a pre-registered-style prediction (burden on non-users) and reports it failing, and it argues for evaluating at market level with sellers allowed to adapt. Results come from a simulated market with LLM agents, so they are hypotheses about mechanisms, not evidence about real markets. Builders of agentic policy simulations can reuse the frozen-versus-adaptive-counterparty comparison.
