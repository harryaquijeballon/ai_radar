# Artificial Intelligence and Economic Growth

## Summary

Aghion, Jones and Jones model AI as a continuation of two centuries of automation, in both the production of goods and services and the production of *ideas*, and ask what this implies for growth and factor shares. The paper's central mechanism is Baumol's cost disease in reverse gear: as AI automates more tasks, growth becomes constrained "not by what we do well but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve" — the hard-to-automate remainder ends up dominating. Under this logic, even rapid AI progress can coexist with modest aggregate growth, while genuinely explosive growth (including singularity scenarios, which the paper treats formally) requires automation of the idea-production process itself. The paper also discusses how firm organization and market structure mediate AI's macro effects.

## Why it matters

The theoretical counterweight to naive extrapolation in AI-growth debates: it explains *why* micro-level capability gains need not show up as macro growth — the same tension the library's OECD G7 entry quantifies and the Imas commentary observes empirically. When policy discussions invoke "AI will transform growth," this is the framework for asking which tasks remain essential and hard to improve.
