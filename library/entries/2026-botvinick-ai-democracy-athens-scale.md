# What Will You Build For: Matthew Botvinick — On AI, Democracy, and "Ancient Athens at Scale"

## Summary

An interview in Cosmos Institute's "What Will You Build For" series with Matthew
Botvinick — a Yale Law senior fellow and Cosmos Institute senior research
fellow who leads AI-and-rule-of-law work at Anthropic, speaking in a personal
capacity. Botvinick argues AI does not create new threats to democracy so much
as it magnifies existing ones: economic insecurity that fuels populism,
national-security emergencies used to expand executive power, and the spread
of state surveillance. He warns that democracies could converge toward a
China-style surveillance model, and that if AI erodes labor-based taxation,
governments risk losing a key channel of accountability to citizens
(unverified: the causal claim that eroded labor taxation directly reduces
government accountability is asserted, not empirically demonstrated in the
piece). He proposes "Athens at scale" — using AI to run deliberative citizen
assemblies at a scale previously infeasible — and names concrete institutional
reforms: statutory limits on surveillance expansion, and treating government
AI prompts and outputs as federal records subject to disclosure. He argues
alignment research and regulation alone cannot resolve these problems, and
that political theory — specifically Philip Pettit's concept of
non-domination — should inform how AI systems are built and governed.

## Why it matters

Gives social-science and policy readers a named, citable framework (Pettit's
non-domination; "Athens at scale") for a question current AI-governance
debates mostly treat informally: what makes AI politically safe for a
democracy, beyond alignment and regulation. The two named institutional
reforms — surveillance-expansion limits and federal-records status for
government AI prompts/outputs — are concrete enough to be cited or argued
against in policy work, not just general concern about AI and democracy.
