# The Capability Transfer: Cohort Reversal, Codified Expertise, and the Adaptability Tax of AI Transformation

## Summary

Extends a single-worker AI-exposure framework (attributed to "Caosun and
Aral, 2026") to a firm with junior and senior cohorts. Because junior work
is the most codified, the model claims junior labour demand falls first,
while the senior stock falls later and further as fewer juniors become
replacement experts. In the paper's baseline calibration, junior demand
drops about 20% below 2022 levels before mostly recovering, while the
senior stock falls about 25% and later halves. Expertise is modelled as
forming mainly through unassisted practice on tasks AI cannot do, so the
time to develop an internal expert is reported to rise from about five
years to nine. The paper argues the costs of this fall on a different
party than the one making the adoption decision: a manager with a
three-year horizon optimizing near-term output cuts the wage bill by 21%,
but the firm's capacity on non-automatable work falls by two-fifths, with
that output going unserved once the pipeline thins (figures as stated in
the fetched abstract; underlying calibration not independently reviewed).
The author explicitly flags two key parameters as unmeasured, framing the
results as scenarios rather than forecasts, and proposes three indicators
for organizations to track: unaided practice time junior staff get on
non-automatable work, senior time spent on such work, and years needed to
develop an expert.

## Why it matters

A concrete, citable model — with explicit, falsifiable mechanisms and
proposed measurement indicators — for researchers and organizations
assessing AI's longer-run effect on human-capital formation and firm
productivity, distinct from the usual task-exposure or wage-effect
framings. The three proposed tracking indicators give practitioners a
specific, actionable starting point rather than a general warning about
"deskilling."
