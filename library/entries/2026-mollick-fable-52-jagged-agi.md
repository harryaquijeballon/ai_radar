# Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI

## Summary
Mollick's assessment of Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2, using a deliberately
narrow working definition of AGI — an AI that matches most human experts at
most intellectual tasks doable on a computer — which he judges the model
broadly meets, terming the result "Jagged AGI" because its weaknesses are
harder to spot than earlier models' obvious factual errors; its main
limitation is that it does not reliably recognize when its own work is
weak. Several self-reported, non-independently-verified examples
(unverified): the model reverse-engineered a USB keyboard and built games
for it from one prompt; given a goal of $20,000/year with ~20 minutes/month
of Mollick's time, it screened 22 business ideas and proposed a paid
service tracking publicly funded infrastructure projects, drawing on
agendas from 201 local governments, estimating itself a 20–40% success
chance; it produced a 182-page paper ("The One-Dollar Company") estimating
that a Colorado LLC-filing-fee cut raised registrations by about 17% while
a UK fee increase lowered them by a similar margin — not preregistered and
explicitly flagged by Mollick as needing human verification; and a
multi-agent "Portals" project generated 450+ 3D worlds via an estimated
25,000 agent runs, whose internal rulebook grew to 204 sections/87,500
words and slowed output to about a dozen worlds/day before separate
"consultant" agents restored throughput to roughly 30+/day.

## Why it matters
For the social-science audience: a vivid, concrete illustration of lens
6's core caution — an AI system can produce a lengthy, plausible-looking
empirical economics paper (natural-experiment design, named effect sizes)
entirely unprompted and unsupervised, with no preregistration and no
independent check, which is exactly the credibility risk the profile exists
to flag rather than a validated result to cite. For the AI-engineering
audience: the "Portals" episode is a concrete, quantified case of emergent
multi-agent bureaucracy — a shared rulebook growing large enough to throttle
the system's own throughput, requiring a second layer of agents to recover
it — directly relevant to anyone designing coordination or context-sharing
mechanisms for long-running multi-agent systems.
