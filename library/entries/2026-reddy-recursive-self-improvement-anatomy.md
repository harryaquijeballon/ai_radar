# Recursive, Self, Improvement: The Anatomy of an Intelligence Explosion

## Summary
Argues that current AI development shows feedback loops resembling I.J.
Good's 1965 "intelligence explosion," but that Eliezer Yudkowsky's model of
recursive self-improvement (RSI) — a model self-modifying its own code —
no longer fits how deep learning actually improves. The essay decomposes
RSI into three separable questions: is the process *recursive* (does each
gain make further gains easier)? what supplies the *self* (not one model,
but the whole AI-development system that trains successive models)? and are
the gains real *improvement*? It identifies two reinforcing feedback
channels — algorithmic progress and hardware investment — that have driven
acceleration for years, and argues there is no single threshold at which
RSI "begins"; forecasts that tracked only compute or only algorithms
underestimated progress because each channel accelerated the other. The
essay's recommendation is to describe the trend as an acceleration of AI
development that can compress timelines dramatically without a sudden,
self-contained "FOOM." It argues this more distributed, less sudden process
offers more warning shots and intervention points than Yudkowsky's model
implies, while noting a capability lead could still compound into
concentrated power over compute and capital — leaving who controls the
system as the open question (unverified: the essay's own synthesis and
argument, not an empirical claim requiring source corroboration).

## Why it matters
Gives researchers and policy analysts reasoning about AI-acceleration risk a
named three-part framework (recursive / self / improvement) for separating
"is progress speeding up" from "is a specific self-modifying mechanism
driving it," useful for assessing whether intervention-point arguments
premised on a sudden single-system takeoff still apply when acceleration is
distributed across an AI-development ecosystem rather than one model.
