# Adopting Fast and Slow: Cross-Country Evidence on Business Adoption of Artificial Intelligence

## Summary

A World Bank background paper for the **World Development Report 2026: The Promise of Artificial Intelligence**, presenting "ten stylized facts on the use of Artificial Intelligence drawn from nationally representative surveys of 4,205 firms in seven economies: India, Jordan, Kenya, Mexico, Nigeria, Thailand, and the United States" (formal-sector firms with five or more employees).

Headline findings as stated:

- **Fast but uneven diffusion.** In the surveyed developing economies, the share of firms using AI rose "from 1 percent in 2020 to 27 percent by 2025"; US adoption surpassed 40 percent in the same period. Firm adoption rates "nearly tripled between 2024 and 2025." Diffusion is faster than earlier digital technologies — website/social-media adoption "took about 15 years to double from 23 to 56 percent" — which the authors attribute to AI's low marginal adoption cost conditional on basic digital infrastructure.
- **Large cross-country variance.** More than 40 percent of Nigerian firms use AI (50–60 percent employment-weighted) against 12 percent in Thailand; the US sits at 43 percent, "comparable to the level seen at the top end of the surveyed developing countries."
- **A sophistication gap on top of the adoption gap.** Chatbots and generative tools dominate usage, consistent with AI being a general-purpose technology. But on the paper's composite sophistication index (frontier applications, customisation, number of business functions, etc.), the US "is about 70 percent higher than in the surveyed developing countries," and sophisticated use is "highly concentrated in large firms."
- **Adoption follows prior capability.** Firms with a product/service-innovation history are 17 percentage points more likely to use AI; R&D investors 13 pp; firms where more than half of workers use computers 35 pp. Lack of knowledge and limited information remain important constraints (Finding 8).
- **Modest early labor-market effects, asymmetric by income level.** Among AI-adopting firms, 16.2 percent of US firms report AI-related layoffs versus 7.4 percent in the surveyed developing countries; hiring slowdowns/freezes 25.2 vs 12.8 percent. Reassignment is more common in developing countries (35.2 vs 16.3 percent), and 20.9 percent of developing-country firms *hired* workers due to AI — "four times the level observed in the U.S." The authors read this as consistent with lower task exposure, less sophisticated use, possible adjustment lags, and task reinstatement in the Acemoglu–Restrepo sense.
- **Optimistic but highly uncertain expectations.** Under the most-likely three-year scenario, developing-country firms expect productivity +10.7 percent and employment +7.2 percent (US: +7.0 and +5.2). Scenario ranges are wide — developing-country productivity expectations span +2 to +21 percent across less-rapid/more-rapid scenarios.

The paper positions itself as among the first studies with comparable firm-level AI data across regions and income groups, an early developing-vs-US differential analysis, and early evidence on AI and labor markets in developing economies — explicitly noting that prior survey evidence "focused on the U.S. and a handful of high-income countries" and that the gap "is particularly acute for developing countries."

## Why it matters

This is the reference cross-country firm-level dataset for arguing about AI diffusion in developing economies — the evidence base lens 2 has lacked. Three usable framings stand out. First, the two-gap structure: developing economies are closing the *adoption* gap (27 percent and tripling) while the *sophistication* gap (~70 percent) persists and concentrates in large firms — a leapfrogging claim and its limit in one dataset. Second, the labor-market asymmetry: displacement is so far a high-income phenomenon (US layoffs at 2× developing-country rates) while developing-country firms show net reassignment and hiring — directly relevant to how AI-and-work policy arguments should differ by income level. Third, for connectivity work (lens 3): the finding that adoption is conditional on basic digital infrastructure and prior digital use (the +35 pp computer-use margin) makes the connectivity agenda a quantified precondition of the AI agenda.

As a WDR 2026 background paper, its numbers will likely anchor the flagship report's narrative — worth citing directly, and worth watching for the WDR itself.
