# Can AI Make Scientific Breakthroughs? Tacit Knowledge is Essential for Discovery

## Summary

An essay arguing that AI cannot presently produce genuine scientific breakthroughs, despite access to the published literature, because research depends fundamentally on *tacit knowledge* — embodied, practical, socially transmitted expertise that exists largely outside the published record.

The authors critique what they characterise as a narrow "read-generate-hypothesis-test-write" model of research as an oversimplification, holding that real discovery is "a social, complex and often inefficient process" resistant to linear automation.

Their central device is an iceberg metaphor distinguishing three layers of scientific knowledge: published papers (the visible tip), grey literature (only partially digitised), and tacit knowledge (held in living memory and undocumented). They note that "only some 35% of the publications indexed... are Open Access," so the corpus available for training is substantially incomplete even at the documented layer. The load-bearing claim is captured in the quotation: "What you publish in an article is always enough to show that you've done it, but never enough to enable anyone else to do it."

Two supporting arguments follow. First, documented failure is absent from the formal record yet essential to expertise, and circulates instead through informal networks — "talking to technicians over lunch," discussion at conferences. Second, the authors explain the asymmetry in AI's recent record by degree of formalisation: mathematics has seen genuine AI progress (they cite the solving of Erdős problems) because it is "among the most formalized and best documented" disciplines, allowing machines to iterate and learn through attempted proofs, whereas fields such as biology are not.

## Why it matters

This supplies a testable predictor rather than a general caution: the more formalised and completely documented a field, the further automated research pipelines should get. That is directly usable when judging claims about AI-automated research — and it implies the demonstrations most often cited as evidence (mathematics, code) are drawn from precisely the domains where the argument predicts success, and so are weak evidence for the fields where it predicts failure.

For a research organisation assessing where to apply AI to its own work, the practical reading is that AI-assisted synthesis should be expected to perform best where methods are standardised and documented, and to degrade where the operative knowledge lives in practitioners rather than papers. The 35% open-access figure is a concrete handle on the size of the documented-corpus gap, useful when arguing about what a model can be presumed to have learned.
