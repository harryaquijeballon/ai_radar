# Algorithm-Driven Information Similarity and Collective Action: An Experimental Study

## Summary

An experimental study of how shared/similar information — the kind produced
by algorithmic curation — affects people's ability to coordinate collective
action. Using a content-moderation game in which participants decide
whether to pay a cost to report harmful content that disappears only once
enough reports accumulate, the authors vary how similar participants'
information is. They find information similarity has opposing effects
depending on the reporting threshold: "more similar information impedes
reporting when few reports suffice and facilitates it when many are
required," a 17-percentage-point reduction in easy (low-threshold)
conditions versus a 34-percentage-point increase in demanding
(high-threshold) ones. The authors attribute the reversal to miscalibrated
beliefs — participants systematically overestimate their own pivotal role
across conditions, with perceived and actual pivotality aligning only at
intermediate thresholds. The results are reported as confirming theoretical
predictions from Basak, Deb, and Kuvalekar (2026) on information
similarity's effects on participation and welfare.

## Why it matters

A mechanism-level account, not just a correlation, for a pattern platform
designers and regulators repeatedly encounter informally: algorithmic
curation that makes users' information environments more similar can
either suppress or amplify collective reporting/moderation behaviour
depending on how much collective effort a given threshold requires — useful
for anyone modelling or designing content-moderation thresholds, or arguing
about platform algorithmic effects on collective political/civic action.
