# Burning the Bridge: Agentification and Domestic Value Added

## Summary

Levy Yeyati models a small open economy in which both exporters and local service providers adopt foreign AI agents. Once wages hit a reservation floor, expanding agent use in local services reduces domestic income even when foreign agent costs stay flat, and value added and consumption can fall further if suppliers of the AI agents capture enough of the resulting cost savings. Exporters can partly offset this: adopting agents can expand their production and earn the foreign exchange needed to pay for further agent use, unlike purely domestic service providers. Using Brazilian national-accounts data, the paper finds that a supplier bargaining share above about 0.15 is enough for further local-service agent adoption to reduce domestic value added, versus about 0.66 for the tradables (exporting) sector — a large asymmetry between the two sectors' exposure. The paper derives the export-growth rate required to preserve income and consumption under agentification, illustrates the mechanism with historical data for Brazil and Mexico, and notes that as domestic capabilities decline, the cost of reverting to local production rises, letting foreign agent suppliers charge more at renewal.

## Why it matters

A specific, quantified mechanism and threshold (the 0.15 vs. 0.66 bargaining-share cutoffs) for when AI-agent adoption helps or harms a developing or emerging economy's domestic value added — directly usable by researchers assessing which sectors of a given economy are more exposed to this "burning the bridge" dynamic, and a concrete framework for the broader debate about whether AI-driven outsourcing of services locks economies into dependence on foreign AI-agent suppliers.
