# Social-science interest profile

> **Strawman draft — edit me.** Drafted by the agent from the project's requirements; the user's editing pass is the quality gate. Scoring behavior changes only when this file changes.

Multidisciplinary scope: economics first, with sociology, political science, and policy research in scope whenever AI or digital technology is the connecting thread. Audience for outputs: economist and social-science colleagues.

## How tiers are decided

- **high** — squarely on at least one lens below AND carries a practical implication: a number, method, dataset, framework, or workflow a researcher could cite, apply, or build on.
- **medium** — on-lens but general, incremental, or indirect: useful context, no immediate application.
- **low** — adjacent background; tangentially informative.
- **n/a** — off-profile. General AI news with no actionable implication for these lenses never scores above low, regardless of how prominent it is.

## Relevance lenses

1. **Economic growth and productivity** — captures: AI/digital effects on growth, TFP and labour productivity; macro projections and their assumptions; productivity measurement debates (J-curve, micro-macro gaps). *High* when it quantifies, models, or measurably challenges how AI affects growth; *medium* for think-pieces restating known positions.

2. **Economic development** — captures: technology diffusion and digital inclusion in developing economies; AI and development outcomes; leapfrogging arguments with evidence. *High* for empirical results or usable frameworks on technology-and-development; *medium* for advocacy without evidence.

3. **Digital technologies and connectivity** — captures: economics of connectivity and digital infrastructure; adoption and usage evidence; spectrum and telecom economics; data/AI infrastructure as economic inputs. *High* when it produces evidence or methods applicable to connectivity/digital-economy analysis.

4. **Competition, regulation and public policy** — captures: AI and digital-market regulation, competition economics of AI (compute, foundation models, platforms), policy evaluation of digital interventions. *High* for analysis that changes how a policy question would be argued or evaluated; *medium* for regulatory news without analysis.

5. **Causal inference and empirical methods** — captures: advances in difference-in-differences, synthetic control, ML-assisted causal inference; LLMs as research instruments (measurement, text-as-data, simulated subjects) including their validity problems. *High* for methods usable in applied work, or credible warnings about method failure modes.

6. **AI applied to social-science research** — captures: generative AI and agentic systems doing economics, sociology, political science, or policy research — simulations of economic agents, AI-assisted literature synthesis, automated empirical pipelines — with attention to credibility and validation, not novelty. *High* when the application is concrete and evaluable; *medium* for demos without validation.

7. **Political science** — captures: AI and digital technologies' effects on political behavior, institutions, and governance — elections and misinformation, state capacity and algorithmic government, platform power and democratic accountability, the geopolitics of AI and digital infrastructure; computational political science methods. Exists deliberately to counterbalance the economics tilt of lenses 1–4. *High* for empirical or theoretical work that changes how AI's political impacts are understood or governed; *medium* for well-informed commentary; punditry stays low.

8. **Philosophy of AI and trust** — captures: normative and epistemic questions about AI — what makes AI systems trustworthy and when trust is warranted; human autonomy and flourishing in AI-mediated life; the epistemology of AI-generated knowledge (can research built on model outputs be *known* to be right?); ethics of AI use in research and policy. A deliberate counterweight: understanding AI's impacts requires the vocabulary for what those impacts are *for*. *High* for frameworks and arguments that sharpen reasoning about trust, autonomy, or epistemic risk in ways usable in research and policy debates; *medium* for reflective essays; hot takes stay low.
